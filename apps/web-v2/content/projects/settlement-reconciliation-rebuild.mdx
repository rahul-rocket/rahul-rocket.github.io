## Overview

A payments platform settled funds to several thousand merchants daily and
reconciled the result by hand. We replaced the spreadsheet-and-judgment process
with a deterministic matching engine backed by an append-only ledger, so that
every match was explainable and every run was replayable. Manual review dropped
from six hours a day to twenty minutes, and the remaining twenty minutes are
spent on genuine exceptions rather than on finding them.

## The Problem

Settlement is the step where money that has been *promised* is checked against
money that has *moved*. A payment processor sends a daily file of transfers; the
platform holds its own record of what each merchant was owed; and reconciliation
is the work of matching one against the other and explaining every line that
does not match. A line that does not match is called a break, and breaks are
where both fraud and bugs surface first.

Two analysts did this in a spreadsheet. The process took about six hours a day
and it was not merely slow, it was *unreliable in a specific direction*: it
found the large breaks and missed the small ones, because a human scanning
fourteen thousand rows tunes out anything under a rounding threshold. Three
percent of lines were unmatched at month end, and the accumulated small
unmatched lines were the ones that appeared in an auditor's sample.

Three things made it worse than a staffing problem. Retries were invisible: a
transfer retried by the processor appeared twice, and nothing in the process
distinguished a duplicate from a genuine second payment. The matching rules
existed only in the analysts' heads, so the same break could be resolved two
ways on two days. And there was no way to answer "why was this matched?" after
the fact, which meant that every audit question became a reconstruction rather
than a lookup.

The business framing was a headcount request. The engineering framing was that
the platform had no *record* of its own settlement reasoning, only an outcome —
and an outcome without its reasoning cannot be checked, cannot be replayed, and
cannot be automated safely.

## Constraints

- **A regulated domain.** A wrong number is an audit finding, not a bug report,
  and "we will fix it forward" is not available.
- **No pause in settlement.** Merchants are paid daily; the migration had to run
  alongside the existing process, not instead of it.
- **The processor's file format was not negotiable** and changed twice a year
  without much warning.
- **A team of six**, of whom two were new to the domain.
- **Every automated match had to be explainable** to a non-engineer, in writing,
  after the fact.

## Options Considered

**Buy a reconciliation product.** Several exist, they are mature, and they solve
this exact problem. Two things ruled it out: the platform's own settlement model
had domain-specific netting rules that would have had to be expressed as
configuration in someone else's vocabulary, and the audit requirement meant we
would have been explaining a vendor's matching decisions rather than our own.

**Automate the spreadsheet.** A script that applied the analysts' rules to the
same two files. Cheap, fast to deliver, and it would have inherited the actual
defect: the rules were undocumented and inconsistent, so automating them would
have made the inconsistency faster and harder to see.

**A deterministic matching engine over an append-only ledger.** Model both sides
as immutable entries, apply an ordered set of named matching strategies, and
record which strategy matched each pair. More work, and it makes the reasoning a
first-class artifact.

**A machine-learning matcher.** Raised, and rejected in one meeting. A
probabilistic match is unexplainable by construction, which fails the single
hardest requirement on the list.

## The Decision

The deterministic engine, with the ledger as the substrate.

The decisive property was not accuracy — the automated rules were about as
accurate as a careful analyst — but *explainability*. Every match records the
strategy that produced it, the two entries it joined, and the run that made it.
An auditor's question becomes a query. An analyst's disagreement becomes a bug
report against a named rule instead of an argument about judgment.

The second property was replay. Because entries are immutable and strategies are
pure functions over them, a run can be re-executed against corrected reference
data and the difference inspected. The old process had no equivalent: correcting
a mistake meant redoing a day's work by hand.

**The cost of this was** roughly three months of additional delivery time
against the automate-the-spreadsheet option, and an ongoing obligation: every
new settlement product now needs its matching strategy written and tested before
it can launch, which has twice delayed a product by a sprint. That is a real
tax, and it is the correct one to pay in this domain — but it is a tax, and the
team feels it.

## Implementation Highlights

**Idempotency keys derived from the data, not generated.** Every ledger entry's
key is a hash of the natural identifiers the source already carries — processor
reference, merchant, amount, value date. A file re-ingested after a failed run
produces the same keys and inserts nothing, so "run it again" became a safe
operation rather than a decision requiring a database backup.

```ts
const ledgerKey = (entry: SourceEntry) =>
  createHash('sha256')
    .update([entry.source, entry.reference, entry.merchantId,
             entry.amountMinor, entry.valueDate].join('|'))
    .digest('hex')
```

**Strategies as an ordered list of pure functions.** Each strategy takes the
unmatched sets and returns candidate pairs; the engine applies them in order and
removes matched entries before the next runs. Ordering is the entire semantics —
exact reference match must run before any fuzzy amount match, or a coincidental
amount collision wins over the certain answer. That ordering is asserted by a
test, because it is the kind of thing a refactor reorders without noticing.

**A regression corpus rather than fixtures.** Six months of anonymised historical
files became a test corpus, and every strategy change is measured against it:
how many lines matched, how many differently, and which. That is how we found
that a proposed date-window widening would have matched 340 more lines and 12 of
them wrongly.

## Challenges & Solutions

**The processor's file changed format mid-project.** A column was added and two
were reordered, with a week's notice. The normalisation layer had been written
as a parser tied to positions; it was rewritten to key off the header row and to
fail loudly on an unrecognised column rather than shifting silently. The failure
mode we avoided is the one that matters: a positional parser reading a reordered
file produces plausible, wrong data and no error.

**Analysts did not trust it, correctly, for six weeks.** We ran both processes in
parallel and compared outputs daily. The engine and the humans disagreed on
about forty lines a day at first; every disagreement was triaged, and roughly a
third of them were the engine being right about something the manual process had
been quietly getting wrong for years. Running in parallel cost six weeks of
duplicated effort and was the only thing that produced trust.

**A replay that was not actually deterministic.** An early strategy sorted
candidates by a timestamp with second precision, and same-second entries came
back in database order — so two replays of one input could produce two different
matchings. Found by running the corpus twice and diffing, which is now part of
the test suite.

The twenty remaining minutes are the point. They are spent on exceptions the

exceptions in a spreadsheet — which is a different job that happens to take a
similar amount of time.

## What I'd Do Differently

I would build the exception workflow first and the matching engine second. We
delivered excellent matching and a rudimentary exception queue, and for the first
two months the analysts' experience was that the tool had automated the easy part
and left them with a worse interface for the hard part. The queue was rewritten
in month three and should have been designed in month one.

I would also resist the ledger's gravitational pull. Because it was immutable,
explainable and replayable, it became attractive as a general event store, and
two unrelated features were built on top of it that had no business being
coupled to settlement. Unwinding one of those took a quarter. A component that
is good at its job invites use that its guarantees were never designed for, and
saying no to that is part of owning it.
